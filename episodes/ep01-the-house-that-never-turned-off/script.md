# EPISODE 1: THE HOUSE THAT NEVER TURNED OFF

**Status:** Complete rewrite draft<br>
**Runtime:** ~14–16 minutes<br>
**Date:** October 12–13, 2013<br>
**Location:** 1842 Harrow Creek Road, Blackwood Valley, Washington<br>
**Season position:** Entry 1 of 13 anchor sites; Carol's planned stay is about eight weeks.

---

## ACT I — THE RV

**INT. WINNEBAGO — NIGHT.** Rain works steadily at the roof. The heater hums. A quiet guitar track plays from the radio.

Carol has made the narrow fold-down table into a field desk. A paper map of Blackwood County is pinned down with a camera lens cap. Thirteen red circles mark the sites she intends to document. The largest cluster follows the main road; one circle sits east of town, in another state. Beside the map: a county evacuation sheet, a state air-monitoring notice, her microcassette recorder, and one roll of film.

The evacuation sheet reads: **BLACKWOOD VALLEY — ESTIMATED POPULATION: 3,500 — HOUSEHOLDS: APPROX. 1,200.**

The state notice is dated 2010. Air monitoring has cleared the contamination concern. In smaller print, the evacuation order remains active pending a separate county action.

Carol checks the camera. A Mamiya RB67. Twelve exposures. She seats the roll, advances it, and checks the heavy strobe. Then she switches on the rechargeable LED lantern. Its light is even, clean, and bright.

She sets the microcassette recorder to RECORD. Tape hiss.

**CAROL:** October twelfth, 2013. Eight forty-six PM. Blackwood Valley, Washington. The county estimated thirty-five hundred people here before the evacuation — about twelve hundred households. Now: nobody.

She looks at the map. One red circle is far east, in Ohio. The note beside it reads: *Pine Valley — check E. Cross's blog first.* She turns the map over before the camera can see the writing.

**CAROL:** I've got about eight weeks, thirteen places I want to see, and one roll for each stop. The order is mostly weather and road access. Tonight is one house. Tomorrow, the school.

She glances at the state notice.

**CAROL:** The air was cleared three years ago. The evacuation order wasn't. That's the kind of sentence you only get when one department finishes its job and another one forgets it has one.

She shuts off the radio. Rain fills the space it leaves.

Carol pulls on her waxed-canvas cloak, hangs the camera around her neck, takes the lantern and recorder, and opens the RV door.

**CUT TO:**

## ACT II — THE PORCH

The old Winnebago sits crooked beside a dark two-story house. Its chevrons have faded almost to gray. Rust blooms around the wheel wells; the side mirror is held on with a bolt. The roof dish points into the rain. A small green light blinks.

Carol clicks RECORD again.

**CAROL:** Entry one. 1842 Harrow Creek Road. One of the twelve hundred, give or take. The county sheet doesn't tell me who lived in which house. That's what the rooms are for.

She leaves the door open behind her, a wedge of warm RV light across the wet grass.

The porch sags in the middle. Rain pours from its low corner in a steady rope. Peeling white columns flank a rusted iron grille. A metal chair has lost its canvas seat; one arm is polished smooth by years of a hand resting there.

A child's red bicycle lies on its side by the steps. Its streamers are stiff and gray. The chain has fused to the sprocket. The front tire is flat and split.

Carol lowers the lantern.

**CAROL:** Kid's bike. The streamers are still tied on. That's a very specific thing to leave behind.

She composes the first frame. The strobe goes off with a hard **CLACK-CHHK**. White light floods the porch, then vanishes.

**PHOTO 1 — BICYCLE WITH STREAMERS.**

Carol advances the film. One frame used.

Above the storm door, a brass number: **1842**. Beneath a layer of mildew, a notice is taped to the glass. She brings the lantern close and reads:

> COUNTY OF BLACKWOOD — MANDATORY EVACUATION
>
> EFFECTIVE 0600 HOURS, AUGUST 11, 1980
>
> Take only essential belongings. Do not store perishables.
> Do not return until the order is rescinded.

She doesn't peel it off. She records the wording.

**CAROL:** Thirty-three years. The order's still on the door. The state says the air cleared. The county never closed the loop.

She pushes the door. It resists, then opens with a long wet creak.

## ACT III — THE HALL

Rain drops to a dull roar behind her. Inside, the house has its own sound: television static from the right, a refrigerator compressor from the left, and a slow tick-tock somewhere beyond the kitchen.

Carol stands still and listens. The recorder captures the room tone.

**CAROL:** Television. Refrigerator. Clock. This place has power.

She points the lantern down. A faded orange-and-brown carpet runner crosses the hallway. It is damp, moldy, and worn smooth down the center. The edges curl up from the floor. Her boots sink into dust beside the worn path.

**CAROL:** There was a route through here. Front door to kitchen, kitchen to stairs. You can see where people kept walking, even after the pattern of the carpet almost disappeared.

She raises the camera and fires. The flash shows the runner, the wet stain near the door, and a row of family photographs on the wall.

**PHOTO 2 — HALLWAY RUNNER.**

At the end of the hall: a rotary phone, unopened mail, keys in a ceramic bowl. A man's work jacket and two small raincoats hang from the rack. Carol looks at the coats, then lowers the camera without taking another shot.

**CAROL:** Nobody came back for those.

She turns into the living room. A CRT television plays gray static. The screen throws a cold, wavering light over a moldy couch and a rotting coffee table. An ashtray is full of old cigarette butts. A TV Guide has one corner folded down. A coloring book lies open on the cushion, one crayon still in its box.

Carol waits, listening for a voice in the static. None comes.

**CAROL:** It's not a picture. It's just a channel with nobody on the other end.

The camera flashes.

**PHOTO 3 — TELEVISION AND COUCH.**

In the kitchen, the avocado-green refrigerator shudders through a compressor cycle. Carol puts a gloved hand near the side, not on it. She feels the vibration through the air.

**CAROL:** The couch is coming apart. The fridge is cold. I don't have a good sentence for that yet.

The dining table has four chairs. One is pulled back. Dust rings remain on the placemats where mugs once stood.

The grandfather clock ticks in the corner. Its brass pendulum swings. A cloth-covered power cord runs from its base to the wall.

Carol follows the cord with her lantern.

**CAROL:** Plugged in. That's an explanation for the clock. It doesn't explain why the line still has power.

She frames the clock and fires.

**PHOTO 4 — GRANDFATHER CLOCK.**

Four frames used. The clock keeps ticking.

## ACT IV — THE STUDY

The study smells of wet wood and old paper. Water-damaged books sag on the shelves. On the desk: a Royal typewriter, newspaper clippings about the Harrow Creek plant, hand-drawn airflow diagrams, and a Geiger counter with a corroded battery compartment.

A leather-bound journal sits beneath the clippings. Carol puts on gloves before opening it. She reads the dated entries without removing the book from the desk.

**INSERT — JOURNAL, OCTOBER 14, 1978:** *The air smells like copper again. Tommy's cough is worse in the mornings. The plant says the readings are normal. I can taste it on my teeth.*

Carol pauses at the date, then reads on.

**INSERT — JOURNAL, MARCH 2, 1979:** *Clearing the back shed. The floor gave way. Found a space underneath. Concrete. Steel door. Civil Defense — built in the sixties. Six berths. It was already stocked when we bought the place. I've started taking the crates down.*

The last entry is dated August 10, 1980.

**INSERT — JOURNAL:** *They're going door to door. Evacuation at six tomorrow. Ruth says I spend more time under that shed than at the table. She's right. I've got ninety days of food down there and six bunks. Tommy and Sammy don't have to breathe that air. We go under when the trucks come. I'll turn the wheel from the inside. We wait it out.*

Carol keeps the journal open and records the entry. She does not read it as a confession or a ghost story. She reads it as a plan someone was frightened enough to make.

**CAROL:** He was going to take his family underground. Four people. Six beds. My guess is he planned to make room for two neighbors.

She turns to the desk's hand-drawn map. It shows the yard and the shed circled twice. The cellar is marked *woodshop*; no shelter is drawn beneath the house.

She photographs the journal pages, then advances the film.

**PHOTO 5 — JOURNAL PAGES.**

She photographs the map.

**PHOTO 6 — HAND-DRAWN MAP.**

Six frames used. Half the roll is gone.

On a shelf behind the desk, a piece of corporate letterhead is half-hidden beneath the ledgers. Carol doesn't see it. The camera does not linger there.

## ACT V — UPSTAIRS

At the far end of the second-floor hall, one bathroom door stands open a few inches. A fluorescent tube buzzes above cracked pink tile. It is the only light upstairs.

Carol stands in the doorway. The mirror has gone cloudy. Rust has run down the tub. The bulb flickers once, then steadies.

**CAROL:** One light in the whole upstairs. Thirty-three years after the evacuation. I know what a bad ballast looks like. I also know that one shouldn't still be on.

She takes the photograph. The strobe and fluorescent flare together, making the small room briefly harsh and bright.

**PHOTO 7 — BATHROOM FLUORESCENT.**

Carol moves through the bedrooms without opening drawers. In Sammy's room, a faded robot comforter covers a twin bed. Plastic soldiers stand under the frame in a battle line that never finished.

On the doorframe, a pencil growth chart carries dates from 1976 to 1979. The marks stop before the next birthday.

Carol studies it for a few seconds, then takes the frame.

**PHOTO 8 — GROWTH CHART.**

At the attic stairs she looks up. The pull-down ladder is already halfway down. One end hangs loose, swaying in the draft.

**CAROL:** Somebody started coming up here and stopped.

She doesn't go farther than the ladder. The attic is wet with ordinary decay: soft boxes, trunks, a yellowed wedding dress, photo albums swollen shut. A height chart is penciled on a joist, starting in 1971.

**CAROL:** The ladder can wait. The floor doesn't look like it wants me on it.

She raises the camera and flashes the boxes from the top rung.

**PHOTO 9 — ATTIC BOXES.**

Nine frames used.

## ACT VI — BELOW THE HOUSE, THEN THE SHED

The basement has no power. Carol takes the narrow stairs with the lantern held low. A cut cord hangs from a bare bulb. The furnace is rusted through. Water has left a line on the cinderblock wall. At the back, a rough plywood partition separates a small woodshop from the rest of the basement.

Three homemade air filters sit beside the workbench: sheet-metal drums, duct-tape seams, stovepipe elbows. Sawdust has turned to felt. The table saw and the tools are red with rust.

Above her, the refrigerator hums.

Carol looks from the black bulb cord to the humming sound overhead.

**CAROL:** Upstairs, the outlets still work. Down here, the air filters and tools have rusted away. The line between those two things isn't on any plan I found.

She photographs the workbench, birdhouses, and filters.

**PHOTO 10 — WOODSHOP AIR FILTERS.**

Ten frames used. Two remain.

Outside, rain hits her cloak hard enough to mask the house sounds. The yard is knee-high with grass. A swing hangs from one chain. The shed door is propped open with a brick.

Inside the shed, a lawnmower has a seized engine. Fertilizer bags have hardened. A workbench holds a dead flashlight. Under a stiff tarp, a steel trapdoor sits flush with the floor. A faded Civil Defense shield is stenciled on the metal.

Carol clears only enough tarp to frame the stencil and pull-ring. She takes the photograph.

**PHOTO 11 — TRAPDOOR AND CIVIL DEFENSE MARK.**

Eleven frames used. One left.

She hooks the iron ring and lifts. The trapdoor groans. Dry air rises from the hole, carrying dust and concrete and nothing else.

Carol shines the lantern down the ladder. Fifteen or twenty feet below, the safety cage and the first rungs are solid. She climbs carefully.

## ACT VII — THE SHELTER

The shelter door swings smoothly on well-greased hinges. Inside: green concrete walls, a swept floor, six metal cots, folded wool blankets, sealed water barrels, old MRE crates, a hand-crank generator, a radio, and children's books. Two of the cots are smaller.

The room is unnaturally quiet. No drip from above. No wind. The lantern stays steady. Carol's arm hair rises with static.

She doesn't touch the supplies. She reads the labels from where she stands.

**CAROL:** Six berths. Ninety days of food. Air schedule on the board. Somebody was ready to keep four people down here for a long time.

On the chalkboard, a neat hand has written:

> AIR — 0600 / 1800
>
> WATER — CHECK
> STOCK — FRIDAY

A wall calendar is still on August 1980. The logbook lies open on the small table, on its last filled page. Carol reads and photographs the entry without taking the book from its place: *Final stock check. Six berths. Ninety days. Water sealed. Filters fresh. All clear.*

**CAROL:** That's the last entry I can find. Nothing says whether the family came down here. Nothing says what happened after the order.

The camera has one exposure left. Carol frames the entire shelter from the doorway: six cots, shelves, calendar, green walls.

She presses the shutter.

The strobe fires blue-purple instead of white. For a fraction of a second, the shadows jump sideways. The tape recorder spits a burst of static. Then the lantern is steady and the room is quiet again.

**PHOTO 12 — THE SHELTER.**

Twelve frames used. The roll is finished.

Carol looks at the camera, then at the recorder.

**CAROL:** That was not the flash I set up.

She stops the tape. Rewinds a few seconds. Plays it back. A sharp static burst lands under the shutter sound. No voice. No second sound. She listens once more and stops the tape.

**CAROL:** I got the frame. I'm going to leave it at that.

## ACT VIII — THE RV, LATER

**INT. WINNEBAGO — AFTER MIDNIGHT.** The rain continues. Carol seals the exposed film in its canister. Before she starts the darkroom work, she checks the recorder and adds a final field note.

**CAROL:** October thirteenth. Twelve fifteen AM. Shelter located, documented, apparently unused. The state cleared the air-monitoring concern in 2010. The evacuation order is still on the books.

She develops the film under the red safelight. The negatives hang from the line. The scanner whirs on the fold-down table.

The twelve scans appear in order: bicycle, runner, static on the television, clock, journal, map, bathroom light, growth chart, attic boxes, woodshop filters, trapdoor, shelter.

The first eleven look as expected. On the last, the shelter has a cold blue cast that Carol did not see by lantern light. She checks the negative under the loupe. The color is there.

She listens to the tape again. Static, shutter, static. Nothing else.

Carol opens the blog editor. A simple masthead reads **LEFT ON**. She types:

> **The House That Never Turned Off — Entry 1**
> The power never got turned off. The television is still on. The fridge is still cold. A clock is still ticking in a dining room no one has used in thirty-three years. The couch, carpet, clothes, and paper have rotted around them. I don't know why the house kept some things and lost the rest.
>
> In a county evacuation sheet, Blackwood Valley is estimated at 3,500 people and about 1,200 households. In 1980 they were ordered out. A state notice says the air was cleared in 2010. The evacuation order was never formally rescinded.
>
> At this house, Daniel Aldridge built a shelter under the shed. Six beds. Ninety days of supplies. I found no sign that his family used it. I also found no record here of where they went. The order says not to return. That's the part that stayed in place.

She pauses, looks again at the last scan, and adds one sentence:

> The last frame came out blue. The flash was set to white. I'll check the camera before the next site.

The satellite dish blinks green. Carol uploads the entry. A progress bar crawls across the screen, then completes.

She checks the map. The next circle is the school.

**CAROL:** Tomorrow.

She closes the laptop. Rain ticks against the roof. The heater hums.

**CUT TO BLACK.**

## CLOSING CARD

Blackwood Valley's estimated pre-evacuation population was 3,500, across approximately 1,200 households. The evacuation order took effect August 11, 1980. Air monitoring was cleared in 2010. The order was never formally rescinded.

**END — ENTRY 1.**
