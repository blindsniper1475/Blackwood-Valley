# EPISODE 1: THE HOUSE THAT NEVER TURNED OFF

## Status: COMPLETE
## Runtime: ~13 minutes
## Date: October 12-13, 2013
## Location: 1842 Harrow Creek Road, Blackwood Valley, WA

---

## ACT I — THE DRIVEWAY

Heavy rain. Not movie rain — the thick, boring, relentless kind that soaks
through a collar in thirty seconds.

An old 1980s Winnebago rolls up the cracked driveway, wipers thwacking,
headlights sweeping across weeds that have fully reclaimed the grass. Faded
sun-bleached chevrons on the side, rust blooming out of the wheel arches, one
side mirror held on with a bolt. Ugly, dented, and running perfectly.

Inside, a low acoustic guitar track drifts out of the radio — something slow
and dusty, barely louder than the engine.

Carol cuts the headlights. Leaves the engine idling.

**CAROL:** "Okay. We're here. Blackwood Valley."

She kills the engine but doesn't move yet. Looks out at the dark shape of the
town beyond the house — rooftops, a water tower, a street that keeps going.
She reaches up and flips a switch on the dash. On the roof, a compact
satellite dish unfolds with a soft mechanical whirr and tilts toward the sky,
a small green light blinking on.

**CAROL:** "Link's up. Home for the next while."

She shuts off the radio. Immediately the rain is the loudest thing in the world.

Behind her, the RV is stocked. Shelves lined with canned food, water jugs,
batteries, a first-aid kit. Taped above the galley is a folded paper map of
Blackwood County, a dozen locations circled in red marker. One circle is far
to the east, in a different state. Next to it, in Carol's handwriting:
"Pine Valley, OH — check E. Cross blog first."

This isn't a weekend. This is a base camp. She came ready to stay.

She shrugs into a heavy waxed-canvas hooded cloak, hangs the medium-format
film camera around her neck — a Mamiya RB67, heavy as a brick, strap worn
soft at the edges — and pulls a rechargeable LED camping lantern off the dash.
She clicks it on. A bright, steady white glow spills out, sharp and even,
cutting clean through the rain-dark. No flame, no flicker. Just a solid,
practical light.

She grabs the microcassette recorder from the passenger seat. Chunky. Physical
buttons. A tape already loaded inside. She hits RECORD. The soft hiss of
magnetic tape fills the cab.

She shuts the RV door. CLANG.

**CAROL:** "October twelfth, 2013. Ten forty-five PM. Eighteen forty-two
Harrow Creek Road, Blackwood County, Washington. Population... currently zero."

A small laugh. Then she looks up at the house.

**CAROL:** "Alright. Let's not stand in the rain."

---

## ACT II — THE COVERED PORCH

The first thing we really see. Slow establishing shot.

A deep covered porch wraps the front of the house, sagging in the middle so
rain sheets off the low corner in a continuous rope. Two thick columns with
peeling white paint. Between them runs a rusted wrought-iron grille —
decorative scrollwork, now flaking orange, the rust bleeding down into the
porch boards in long streaks.

Off to one side sits a rusted metal porch chair, iron frame, the canvas seat
rotted to a lattice of threads. One armrest is bare metal, polished smooth by
decades of a hand resting there.

And leaning against the house beside the steps — a child's bicycle, lying on
its side. A small Murray BMX, red paint almost gone, a few plastic streamers
still knotted to the handlebars, grey and stiff with rust. The chain has fused
to the sprocket. One pedal is bent. The front tire has collapsed flat and
split along the sidewall.

Carol stops in front of it. Lowers the lantern.

**CAROL:** "Okay. There's a bike out here. Kid's bike."

She raises the Mamiya. Lines up the shot. The heavy external strobe is
mounted on top, a bulky rectangle of plastic and glass.

CLACK-CHHK. The shutter fires. The strobe BLASTS — a blinding, physical burst
of white light that illuminates the entire porch for a split second. The
streamers. The bent pedal. The rust. Then darkness rushes back in, and there's
a faint ringing in the ears.

**CAROL:** "See the streamers? Those are still on there. This kid parked his
bike and went inside. And that was thirty-three years ago."

She winds the film advance lever. CLACK. Twelve shots per roll. She's already
used one.

She stands. Moves to the porch steps. They groan under her. There's a rotted
HOME SWEET HOME mat, the letters barely readable, and a warped screen door
with a tear in the mesh big enough to put your hand through. Above it: 1842,
in tarnished brass. The porch light above the door is dark — its glass
cracked, long dead.

Taped to the storm door, under a layer of mildew, is a sheet of paper. She
holds the lantern close.

> COUNTY OF BLACKWOOD — MANDATORY EVACUATION
> EFFECTIVE 0600 HOURS, AUGUST 11, 1980
> Take only essential belongings. Do not attempt to store perishables.
> All utilities will remain in place pending federal inspection.
> Do not return until the order is rescinded.

**CAROL:** "'All utilities will remain in place pending federal inspection.'"

She looks at the camera lens, half-grinning, unhappy about it.

**CAROL:** "That's how you end up with a house that never got turned off."

She pushes the door. It resists. Gives with a long, wet creak.

---

## ACT III — THE HALLWAY AND THE CARPET

The rain drops to a dull roar behind her.

And the house is loud.

A hiss of static from the right. A low, throaty compressor hum from the left.
And underneath both, a steady, patient tick-tock, tick-tock.

Carol doesn't move for a moment. She's listening. The microcassette recorder
hisses softly in her hand, capturing it all.

**CAROL:** "Hang on. Hang on — do you hear that?"

She holds the recorder up, aiming it at the dark like a microphone.

**CAROL:** "That's a television. And a refrigerator. This place has power.
The whole house has power."

Then she looks down, and the camera follows.

A carpet runner runs the full length of the hallway. Faded seventies
rust-orange and brown, a scrolling floral pattern worn almost flat in the
center. It's damp. It smells like wet wool and black mold. The edges have
curled up into little ridges, and where the roof leaks near the front door
there's a dark water stain spreading like a map. Her boots sink into it half
an inch and leave prints in thirty-three years of dust.

She follows it with the lantern. The runner leads straight down the hall, then
turns and climbs the staircase — a matching carpet stair-runner, with two
bare, polished grooves worn into the middle of each tread.

**CAROL:** "Runner's still down. Look at the wear pattern, though. Right down
the middle. That's years of people walking this exact path. Kitchen to front
door. Front door to stairs."

She raises the Mamiya. Lines up the hallway. The strobe FIRES.

CLACK-CHHK. FLASH. For one frame everything is sharp: the peeling floral
wallpaper, the broken banister upstairs, a row of family photographs on the
wall, water-blistered and yellowed. Then the dark rushes back.

She winds the advance lever. CLACK. Two shots used.

On a narrow console table beside the stairs: a rotary telephone, a ceramic
bowl with a set of keys still sitting in it, and a stack of unopened mail gone
soft and grey. A man's work jacket and two small raincoats hang on a wall
rack. Still hanging.

**CAROL:** (quietly) "Nobody went back for their coats."

---

## ACT IV — LIVING ROOM, KITCHEN, DINING

**Right: the living room.**

The CRT television is on. Bulky, wood-grain plastic, a hum of its own, screen
rolling with thick grey static that strobes cold blue-white across the room.
No picture. No signal. Just static, playing to nobody since 1980.

The moldy couch faces it. Fabric split at the seams, foam crumbling out of
one arm. A moth-eaten knitted afghan over the back. The rotting coffee table
in front has an ashtray full of dead cigarette butts, an empty beer bottle,
and a TV Guide with the corner folded down. A coloring book sits on the
cushion, a crayon still in the box.

**CAROL:** "It's just static. There's no broadcast on this channel anymore.
It's been doing this to an empty room for — I don't even have a number for
that."

She lifts the Mamiya. CLACK-CHHK. FLASH. The strobe freezes the couch, the
table, the glowing screen. Three shots used.

She winds the lever. Looks around. Notices something.

**CAROL:** "And look — the couch is completely wrecked. Mold everywhere.
The carpet's rotting. But the TV... the TV is still on. Still getting power.
The wiring's fine. It's like the house is keeping its skeleton alive but
letting everything else go."

**Left: the kitchen and dining area.**

The avocado-green refrigerator shudders through a compressor cycle. The sound
is huge in the quiet house. Beside it, an avocado stove with a kettle fused
to the burner. Checkered linoleum curling at the seams like peeling skin.
Cabinet doors hanging open. Dishes crusted in the sink. A child's drawing
held up on the fridge door by a magnet that has somehow, impossibly, kept
its grip.

She gets close to the fridge. Puts a hand on the side. Feels it vibrate.

**CAROL:** "It's cold. The fridge is still cold."

She pulls her hand back like it bit her.

**CAROL:** "Okay. That's... that's a problem. That compressor should have
seized decades ago. I'm not — I'm not gonna theorize. I'm just gonna note it
and move on."

**The dining area.**

A heavy table, four chairs. One pushed back at an angle, like someone stood
up in a hurry. Dust rings on the placemats where four mugs stood.

And in the corner, the grandfather clock. Ornate dark wood, brass pendulum
swinging, dial glowing faintly amber.

Tick. Tock. Tick. Tock.

**CAROL:** "Oh — the clock's going. The clock is actually going."

She circles it, lantern low, and finds the answer on the floor behind it: a
cloth-covered power cord, snaking into a wall outlet.

**CAROL:** "Electric movement. It's plugged in."

She laughs, but it's not a happy laugh.

**CAROL:** "Of course it is. Everything's plugged in."

CLACK-CHHK. FLASH. The pendulum caught mid-swing. Four shots used.

---

## ACT V — THE STUDY AND THE JOURNAL

Small room off the hall, seventies wood paneling, sagging shelves of swollen
water-damaged books. A heavy oak desk. A Royal typewriter with a sheet still
rolled into it. Newspaper clippings about the Harrow Creek processing plant.
Hand-drawn airflow diagrams. A Geiger counter with a corroded battery
compartment.

Among the clippings, half-hidden under a stack of ledgers, is a piece of
corporate letterhead. Faded. The logo at the top reads:
**WESTFIELD PROPERTIES & LOGISTICS — A Division of Abscissa Industries.**

Carol doesn't notice it. She's focused on the journal.

A leather-bound journal under a stack of ledgers. She brushes the dust off
with two fingers. Opens it.

**CAROL:** "Aldridge. Daniel Aldridge. This is the father's log.
Okay — read with me."

> "October 14, 1978. The air smells like copper again. Tommy's cough is
> worse in the mornings. The plant says the readings are normal. I can
> taste it on my teeth."

She turns pages. The handwriting tightens, the letters crowding together.

> "March 2, 1979. Clearing out the back shed and the floor gave way under
> me. Found a space underneath. Concrete. Steel door. Civil Defense — built
> in the sixties. Six berths. It was already stocked when we bought the
> place. The previous owner never told us. I've started taking the crates
> down."

Later pages. Rough sketches of filter housings. A note in the margin:
CHANGE EVERY 72 HOURS.

The final entry. Pressed hard enough to tear the paper.

> "August 10, 1980. They're going door to door. Evacuation at six tomorrow.
> Ruth says I spend more time under that shed than I do at the table. She's
> right. But I've got ninety days of food down there and six bunks. Tommy
> and Sammy don't have to breathe that air. We go under when the trucks
> come. I'll turn the wheel from the inside. We wait it out."

Silence. Just the clock, somewhere down the hall. The tape recorder hisses.

**CAROL:** "He was going to refuse the evacuation."

She looks up from the page.

**CAROL:** "He was going to seal his family in a hole and wait for the air
to die. And that word he keeps using — 'copper.' The air smells like copper.
That's not... I don't know what chemical smells like copper. That's weird."

CLACK-CHHK. FLASH. The journal pages. Five shots used.

She winds the lever. Pauses. Looks at the hand-drawn map with the shed
circled twice.

CLACK-CHHK. FLASH. The map. Six shots used. Half the roll gone.

**CAROL:** "Right. Upstairs first. Then the shed."

---

## ACT VI — UPSTAIRS

The stair carpet muffles her footsteps, which is worse than the creaking.
She has to listen to feel the wood flexing under her.

Second-floor landing. Four bedrooms, one full hall bathroom, and the master's
own en-suite.

At the far end of the hall, a bathroom door stands cracked open an inch. A
thin blade of sickly yellow-white light falls across the carpet.

A fluorescent tube above the sink is on. Flickering. Buzzing.

It is the only light burning anywhere upstairs.

Carol stands in the doorway for a long moment before going in.

**CAROL:** "One light. One. In a house with thirty-three years of nobody
in it."

Inside: cracked pink tile, mold along the ceiling line, a rust-stained tub,
a mirror desilvered into clouded grey patches that reflect her cloak and the
lantern but not really her face. The tube buzzes like something caught in
a wire.

She raises the Mamiya. CLACK-CHHK. FLASH — and for a second the fluorescent
and the strobe fire together, and the room goes brutally, clinically bright.
Seven shots used.

**CAROL:** "Bulb should have burned out. Ballast should have gone. I'm not —
I'm not staying in here."

**The bedrooms.**

Master: a decaying four-poster, stained mattress, a woman's dress shoes lined
up under the vanity. Her en-suite is dark — clawfoot tub, pink tile, that
ruined mirror.

Tommy's room: single bed, model rocket plans taped to the wall, a cheap
guitar with two strings left, a stack of comics with the spines split.

Sammy's room: a twin bed, a faded robot comforter, plastic soldiers under
the bed in a battle line that never got finished. On the doorframe, a pencil
growth chart with dates and little horizontal ticks. 1976. 1977. 1978. 1979.

It stops in 1980.

Carol stands in front of it. Doesn't raise the camera right away.

**CAROL:** "They measured him in the doorway. That's... you do that, right?
You just do that every year."

Then the camera comes up. CLACK-CHHK. FLASH. Eight shots used.

Fourth bedroom: a guest room that became storage. Good clothes in garment
bags. Christmas tinsel. A sewing machine under a sheet. Boxes labeled in
Ruth's careful handwriting.

---

## ACT VII — THE ATTIC

She's heading for the stairs when she looks up.

The attic hatch is ajar. The pull-down ladder is halfway down — hanging there
in the dark, one end loose, swaying almost imperceptibly in the draft.

**CAROL:** "Wait. That's already down. Partway."

She stops. Looks at it. Looks down the hall at the empty rooms.

**CAROL:** "Somebody pulled it down and didn't finish."

She doesn't resolve it. She reaches up, takes the bottom rung, and pulls.
The ladder slams the rest of the way down. CLATTER. A whole season of dust
rains out of the ceiling and hangs in the lantern beam like snow.

She climbs three rungs. Raises the lantern.

Stacks of cardboard boxes, soft and sagging with damp. Leather trunks.
Furniture under sheets that look like people until the light moves. A wedding
dress in an open trunk, yellowed at the hem. Photo albums with swollen spines.

And on the attic joist, another height chart. Older. Starting in 1971.

**CAROL:** "I'm not going further. The joists are soft and this air is
genuinely bad."

CLACK-CHHK. FLASH, way up in the dark, over the boxes. Nine shots used.

She climbs down. Pushes the ladder up until it catches. Doesn't latch it
all the way.

---

## ACT VIII — THE BASEMENT AND THE WOODSHOP

Down the main stairs, past the kitchen, to the narrow door under the
staircase. Cold damp air rolls out of it as she opens it.

The basement is pitch black — the power was never run down here. Only the
LED lantern. A bare bulb hangs from the ceiling on a cord that's been cut.

Half-finished. Concrete slab under the house, packed dirt at the back.
Exposed joists sweating condensation. A rusted cast-iron furnace. A
seventies Maytag washer and dryer with the lids down. A towering water
heater. A flood line ghosted up the cinderblock wall. Faintly, far above,
she can still hear the refrigerator humming.

**CAROL:** "Okay. No power down here. The stasis — whatever it is — it
doesn't reach the basement. Everything down here is just... normal decay.
Rust. Mold. Water damage. But upstairs, the wiring's still live. The
fridge is still going. It's like the preservation follows the circuits."

At the back: a partition wall. Plywood and rough studs, built later than
the rest of the basement, badly finished, with a plain door in it.

**CAROL:** "Okay. He built a wall down here. Separate room."

She opens it.

A woodworking shop. Workbench buried in a layer of sawdust that's turned to
felt. Half-finished birdhouses. A rusted table saw with a blade still on the
arbor. Stack of lumber. Hand tools hanging on a pegboard, every silhouette
outlined in rust.

And in the corner — three homemade air filters. Sheet metal, duct tape,
stovepipe elbows, great drum-can housings crudely riveted together. Hoses
coiled on the floor.

**CAROL:** "These are his. The ones in the journal. He was building his own
filtration system down here."

She runs a gloved finger along one. Comes away grey.

**CAROL:** "This isn't a crazy man's room. It's a man who was trying to fix
something. That's the part that gets me."

CLACK-CHHK. FLASH across the bench, the birdhouses, the filters.
Ten shots used. Two left.

**CAROL:** "But the shelter isn't down here. It's under the shed.
Coming back up."

---

## ACT IX — THE YARD, THE SHED, THE DESCENT

She steps out the back door and the rain hits her like a wall after the
house. The sound crashes back in, loud and chaotic, and for a moment she
just stands there, letting it wash over the cloak, letting the dead quiet
of the indoors fall away.

The backyard stretches out. Overgrown. The grass is knee-high in places,
brown and matted down. A leaning swing set, one chain snapped, the seat
swinging slowly in the wind with nobody in it. A dead clothesline with one
grey shirt still clipped to it, rotting in the rain. Garden beds gone to
brambles. A rusted wheelbarrow tipped over near a collapsed fence.

And in the far corner, the shed.

It's a small wooden structure, sagging roof, warped walls. The door is
propped open with a brick.

**CAROL:** "He left it open. Probably for ventilation, running those
filters. Needed airflow."

She crosses the yard. The mud sucks at her boots. She reaches the shed and
pushes the door wider.

Inside: rusted tools, a lawnmower with a seized engine, bags of fertilizer
hardened into concrete, a workbench with an old dead flashlight on it.
Cobwebs thick across the ceiling. The floor is concrete, cracked, with a
layer of damp sawdust.

And in the center, half-hidden under a mold-stiff canvas tarp, is a steel
trapdoor, flush with the shed floor.

A faded yellow Civil Defense shield stenciled on the metal. An iron pull-ring.

**CAROL:** "There it is."

She raises the Mamiya. Lines up the shot.

CLACK-CHHK. FLASH catches the logo, the rust, the pull-ring.
Eleven shots used. One left.

She hooks two fingers through the ring. Pulls. The hinges scream — a high,
metallic shriek that cuts through the rain. The trapdoor comes up, heavy,
and she braces it against the wall.

A rush of air comes up out of the hole. Not cold, exactly. Still. Dry. No
smell of rot, no damp. Just dust and concrete and time.

**CAROL:** "The air coming out of that is so dry it feels like it's been
sitting there forever."

She keeps the LED lantern in her left hand. Looks down.

A steep wooden ladder drops fifteen, twenty feet into the earth. A rusted
safety cage around the lower rungs. The wood is old but solid. No rot.
He must have sealed it well.

She climbs down.

The rain fades above her. By the time her boots hit the concrete at the
bottom, there is nothing at all but her breathing and the quiet of the
LED lantern.

---

## ACT X — THE SHELTER

A short concrete corridor, maybe ten feet. Then the steel vault door, heavy,
with a locking wheel on the inside face.

She pushes. The hinges swing — and they're smooth. Well-greased. She notices.
Files it away. Doesn't say anything.

The lantern comes up.

And the room opens out in front of her, and it's pristine.

Fully finished. Concrete walls painted a dull institutional green, not
peeling, not stained. A swept floor. Low ceiling with dark fluorescent
fixtures that haven't been lit in thirty-three years. The air is dry and
still and smells like nothing — no mold, no rot, no damp. Just the faint,
metallic scent of old steel. And something else. Something like ozone.
Like the air before a lightning strike.

**CAROL:** (quietly, reverent) "Oh my god."

She rubs her arm. The hair on her forearm is standing up. Static.

**CAROL:** "It's... it's freezing in here. Not cold like winter. But my
skin is prickling. Like I'm standing too close to a TV screen."

**The cots.** Six metal cots lined up against the back wall. Wool blankets
folded crisp at the foot of each one. Pillows at the head. Two of them are
smaller. Children's cots.

**CAROL:** "Six cots. That's the four of them, plus two. Neighbors. He was
going to let two neighbors in."

**The supplies.** Shelving units, floor to ceiling, along the left wall.
Olive-drab MRE crates, stacked neat. Steel water barrels. Walls of canned
goods, labels still readable. Spare air filters in waxed paper packaging.
A sealed medical kit with a red cross. A hand-crank generator bolted to the
wall. A hand-crank radio on a shelf. Board games. A stack of children's
paperbacks.

**CAROL:** "Look at all of this. MREs, water, generators, filters, medical
kit. He stocked this to the ceiling."

She moves down the shelving, lantern low, reading dates off the crates.

**CAROL:** "1980. All of it's 1980. He spent months on this."

She raises the Mamiya. One shot left. She lines it up carefully. The whole
room. The cots. The shelving. The green walls.

She presses the shutter.

CLACK-CHHK.

But the flash doesn't fire white. It fires a sickly, blue-purple. A cold,
wrong light that makes the shadows jump sideways instead of back. The room
goes violet for a split second, and the hair on Carol's arms stands straight
up, and the tape recorder in her pocket emits a sharp burst of static.

Then it's over. The dark rushes back. The lantern holds steady.

Carol stares at the camera.

**CAROL:** "...What the hell was that?"

She looks at the strobe. Taps it. It seems fine. She looks around the room.
Everything is still. Silent. Perfect.

**CAROL:** "Okay. That was... I'm gonna pretend that didn't happen."

**The far wall.** A chalkboard with a schedule in Daniel's careful handwriting:

> AIR — 0600 / 1800
> WATER — CHECK
> STOCK — FRIDAY

A wall calendar. August 1980. Never turned.

**CAROL:** "August. It's still on August."

**The logbook.** On a small table near the cots, a leather-bound logbook.
She picks it up. Opens it.

One page filled. Neat, careful handwriting. The same hand that wrote the
journal upstairs.

> Final stock check. Six berths. Ninety days. Water sealed. Filters fresh.
> All clear.

Nothing after it. Not one word.

**CAROL:** (reading aloud, slow) "Final stock check. Six berths. Ninety
days. Water sealed. Filters fresh."

She closes the book. Holds it for a second.

**CAROL:** "All clear."

She puts it back on the table. Straightens it. Doesn't photograph it.
Some things she just leaves.

**CAROL:** (to the recorder, quiet) "He never turned the wheel. They
evacuated with everybody else. Same as the rest of the town. And this room
just stayed down here. Fully stocked. Waiting for people who never came
back."

She looks at the two small cots.

**CAROL:** "He built all of this to keep them breathing. And it turns out
he didn't need to. The air cleared. They could have come back. But the
evacuation order was never rescinded. So they didn't. And this place just
waited."

A long beat. The LED lantern holds its light, steady and bright. The
silence is total. No hum. No tick. No static. Just her breathing and the
faint ozone smell.

**CAROL:** "I've got everything. Let's get out of here."

She turns. Walks back through the shelter. Past the cots, past the shelving,
past the calendar frozen on August. She pulls out the microcassette recorder.
Hits STOP. Rewinds. Hits RECORD again.

**CAROL:** "Final log. October thirteenth. Twelve fifteen AM. Shelter
located, documented, fully intact. Never used. The contamination cleared in
2010. The air out there is fine. It's been fine for years."

She looks around once more.

**CAROL:** "But you can feel how afraid he was. You can stand in here and
feel it. He was so sure he was saving them."

She picks up the lantern. Walks to the vault door. Pauses at the threshold.
Looks back at the room.

**CAROL:** "Goodbye, Daniel."

She steps into the corridor. Pulls the vault door shut. It swings on those
well-greased hinges and closes with a deep, resonant thud that she feels
in her chest.

---

## ACT XI — THE OUTRO

She climbs. Twenty feet of ladder, rungs cold under her gloves. Out through
the shed. Pulls the trapdoor shut. The CLANG is enormous, and then the rain
comes crashing back into her ears.

She crosses the yard, past the leaning swing set, through the mud, up the
porch steps. She pauses at the child's bicycle still lying on its side.
Doesn't touch it.

Back through the hallway, over the damp runner. Past the clock still ticking.
Past the couch still watching static. She doesn't take any more photos.
The roll is finished. Twelve shots. All used.

She sets the lantern on the console table beside the bowl of keys.

Then she's out the front door, down the porch steps, and back across the
wet grass to the RV. The little green satellite light blinks on the roof,
waiting.

---

## ACT XII — THE RV, LATER THAT NIGHT (THE DARKROOM RITUAL)

**INT. THE RV — NIGHT**

The RV. Home. The little space is warm, lived-in, organized. Outside the
window the rain has eased to a drizzle. The heater hums. The acoustic track
plays low on the radio.

On the roof, the satellite dish holds its line to the sky, green light
blinking.

Carol moves to the back of the RV. The darkroom section. Blackout curtains
sealed with duct tape. She pulls them tight. Clips on the red safelight.
The whole space goes crimson.

She opens the Mamiya's back. Pulls the exposed film roll out in the red
dark. Loads it onto the developing reel. Slides it into the light-tight tank.

Pours developer. Agitates. The liquid sloshes. She counts under her breath.
Thirty seconds. Stop bath. Fixer. The smell of vinegar and chemicals fills
the tiny space.

She hangs the negatives on the clothesline strung across the ceiling. Binder
clips. Drips falling onto a towel below. She sits. Waits. Heats up a can of
soup on the little stove. Eats standing up, watching the negatives drip.

Once they're dry, she clips a strip onto the light table. The thin white
panel glows up through the film. Tiny inverted worlds. She leans in with the
loupe. Studies each frame. The bicycle. The hallway. The TV. The clock. The
journal. The growth chart. The attic. The filters. The trapdoor. The shelter.

Twelve frames. Twelve shots. Every one of them earned.

She selects the best. Feeds them into the flatbed scanner one by one. The
scanner whirs. The images appear on the laptop screen, inverted, then
corrected. Color returns. The green walls of the shelter. The blue-white
glow of the TV. The orange rust of the grille.

She opens the blog editor. Types.

**We hear her voice reading what she writes, soft, reflective:**

**CAROL (V.O.):**
"Some houses are haunted. This one isn't. It's just... waiting. The power
never got turned off. The clock never stopped. The fridge never died. The TV
has been playing static to an empty room for thirty-three years. And one
light in the upstairs bathroom has been on since 1980, burning steady, like
the house is holding its breath."

She pauses. Types more.

**CAROL (V.O.):**
"Daniel Aldridge built a shelter under his shed. Six cots. Ninety days of
food. Air scrubbers. A chalkboard with a schedule for the kids. He was going
to seal his family inside and wait for the air to clear. He never did. The
town evacuated. They left everything behind. And the shelter just stayed down
there, fully stocked, waiting for people who never came back."

She looks at the scanned photo of the two small cots.

**CAROL (V.O.):**
"He built all of it to keep them breathing. And it turns out he didn't need
to. The air cleared. They could have come home. But the evacuation order was
never rescinded. So they didn't. And the house just kept running. Ticking.
Humming. Watching static."

She sits back. Reads it through. Then her fingers move again.

**CAROL (V.O.):**
"I'm not going to tell you where it is. You don't need to go there. But if
you ever drive through Blackwood County and you see a house with one light
on, second floor, and a bike still lying in the yard... just drive past.
Some places aren't meant to be found. They're meant to be remembered."

She hits a key. The blog post publishes. A small progress bar creases the
corner of the screen — uploading over satellite, slow but steady. The title
appears at the top of the page:

> **THE HOUSE THAT NEVER TURNED OFF**
> Eighteen forty-two Harrow Creek Road. October 2013.
> **Entry 1.**

She watches the upload finish. Closes the laptop. Sits for a moment in the
red glow of the safelight.

Then she reaches over and flips off the red lamp. The little space goes dark
except for the green blink of the satellite light and the faint glow of the
dashboard.

She pulls the curtain across the windshield. Outside, the town waits.

**CAROL (V.O.):** (quiet, to herself)
"Okay. That's one."

She's not going anywhere. Not yet. There's a whole town left.

**CUT TO BLACK.**

> Blackwood Valley was declared free of contamination in 2010.
> The evacuation order was never formally rescinded.
> Four hundred and twelve homes remain sealed.

**END — ENTRY 1.**
